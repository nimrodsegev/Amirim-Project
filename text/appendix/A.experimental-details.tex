\section{Experimental Details and Supporting Analyses}
\label{app:details}

\subsection{Implementation}

Hidden states are extracted with a single forward pass over
\texttt{[BOS]} $+$ context $+$ phrase, with \texttt{output\_hidden\_states}
enabled. The BOS token is required: the state of a phrase's first token at
position $0$ differs substantially from its state at position $1$ after a BOS,
and omitting it produces degenerate patchscope outputs.\footnote{This was the
source of a long-lived bug in an earlier version of this work, in which
patchscope outputs for well-known phrases were unrelated to the input.}
Positions are enumerated from the first phrase token to the second-to-last; the
BOS and the final phrase token are never cut points.

For the Patchscopes readout, the states for all probed layers are stacked into
one batch and decoded in a single \texttt{generate} call. This is one call, not
one sequence's worth of computation: the sweep still decodes one sequence per
layer. Decoding is greedy
throughout, for both readouts, with \texttt{max\_new\_tokens} $= i+3$.

\subsection{Patchscope prompt ablation}

The prompt \texttt{"Repeat this: X"} offers one slot for the injected state.
Because the target is several tokens long, a natural worry is that one slot is
too little room. We reran the isolated-phrase sweep with
\texttt{"Repeat this: X X X X X"}, injecting the same state into five slots.
It does not help, and at longer distances it hurts: $27.4\%$ versus $30.9\%$ at
$i=3$, $15.8\%$ versus $19.4\%$ at $i=4$, and $12.4\%$ versus $16.5\%$ at
$i=5$. We therefore use the single-slot prompt throughout. The failure mode at
long $i$ is not a shortage of output positions.

\subsection{Pooling across layer groups}

A complementary question to where recovery happens is whether the early layers
ever succeed \emph{alone}. Grouping the eight probed layers into
$A=\{5,7\}$, $B=\{10,13,15\}$ and $C=\{20,25,30\}$ and asking which groups
contain at least one succeeding context for each phrase, $47.8\%$ succeed in
all three, $17.3\%$ in $B$ and $C$ only, and $33.7\%$ in $C$ only. These cover
$98.8\%$ of phrases: almost none succeeds early without also succeeding later,
so recovery accumulates with depth rather than appearing and disappearing.

This is a deliberately generous criterion (any of up to ten contexts, at any
layer in the group, counts), so the absolute numbers are much higher than a
single context checked at a single layer would give. Duplicated contexts do not
add to them: an exact repeat cannot turn a failure into a success or create a
new group membership. The ordering is the informative part, not the levels.

\subsection{Probe configuration}

Both probes use an MLP with a single hidden layer of $32$ units, trained on the
standardised hidden state at the cut position. Data are pooled over
$i \in \{2,3,4,5\}$ and split $80/20$ with \texttt{GroupShuffleSplit} grouped by
phrase, so no phrase appears in both partitions; the majority class is
downsampled in the training set only, and the test set is left at its natural
distribution. All reported runs use seed $42$. The false-positive and
confidence analyses of \S\ref{sec:falsepos} use the L25 probe.

\subsection{The L2 irregularity under the Patchscopes label}

The generation-label probe climbs smoothly through the earliest layers
(Figure~\ref{fig:early}); the Patchscopes-label probe on the \emph{same}
states does not, reaching $76.6\%$ balanced accuracy at L1, dropping to
$55.4\%$ at L2 and recovering to $76.0\%$ at L5. We doubt the dip is a property
of layer 2: Patchscopes itself almost never succeeds this early ($0.7\%$,
$0.8\%$ and $1.6\%$ at L0, L1 and L2), so the positive class is
$0.6$--$1.2\%$ of the data, roughly $100$ to $250$ examples, and one split can
swing on luck. The generation label, whose positive rate is a fixed $69.1\%$ at
every layer, shows no such irregularity on the same states. No multi-seed check
was run, so we report it as an unresolved artifact rather than a finding.

L1 is worth flagging in the other direction: $76.6\%$ balanced accuracy after a
single transformer block, on a label Patchscopes can satisfy less than $1\%$ of
the time at that depth.

\subsection{Probe generalisation to unseen categories}

Training the generation-label probe on two phrase categories and testing on the
third costs accuracy but does not eliminate the signal. Against a no-hold-out
baseline of $73.6\%$ / $74.6\%$ / $76.8\%$ balanced accuracy at L13 / L25 / L30,
holding out landmarks gives $69.1\%$ / $62.6\%$ / $63.4\%$, idioms
$68.7\%$ / $68.9\%$ / $72.3\%$, and film titles
$72.4\%$ / $72.7\%$ / $73.5\%$. Landmarks are the hardest category to
generalise to, which is unsurprising given that they are only $5.7\%$ of pooled
instances. The probe is therefore learning something that partly transfers
across phrase types rather than memorising category-specific surface features,
but the transfer is imperfect, a pattern also reported by
\citet{orgad-etal-2025-llms} for truthfulness probes across datasets.

\subsection{Per-phrase correlation: robustness}

Figure~\ref{fig:corr} uses the balanced MLP probe at $i=3$ over $180$ test
phrases, but the shape is not specific to it. Across the four combinations of
classifier (logistic, MLP) and training regime (natural, class-balanced), the
L13 correlation ranges from $0.444$ to $0.584$ on $i=3$ and $0.432$ to $0.564$
pooled, the MLP consistently ahead of logistic regression and balanced training
ahead of natural; L25 preserves the ordering at lower values, $0.381$ to
$0.531$.\footnote{Re-running this
sweep reproduced the balanced MLP value at L25 to four decimal places
($0.5173$), but returned $0.499$ for the natural-training MLP against the
$0.531$ recorded at the time, most likely package-version drift. We report the
recorded values and flag the one that did not reproduce; the ordering across
variants, which is what the paragraph claims, is unaffected.} Across five random
phrase-level splits the L13 balanced logistic probe gives AUC-ROC between
$0.806$ and $0.828$, so the split contributes little of the variation.

\subsection{Full probe metrics}

\input{tables/probes}

Table~\ref{tab:probes} gives balanced accuracy and precision at every probed
layer, the two measures we rely on. For completeness, the generation-label probe's AUC-ROC rises from
$0.76$ (L5) to $0.83$ (L30) and its AUC-PR from $0.85$ to $0.91$ against a base
rate of $0.69$, both monotone in depth. The patchscopes-label probe inverts this:
AUC-ROC is highest at L5 ($0.89$) and lowest at L25 and L30 ($0.74$), the
opposite ordering to its balanced accuracy. The two metrics disagree because
they answer different questions (ranking quality versus thresholded decision
quality) under a positive rate that moves from $6.6\%$ to $47.7\%$ across
layers. On $F_1$ the generation-label probe only beats the trivial
always-predict-success baseline ($81.7$) at L30 ($82.9$), because that baseline
buys perfect recall for free on a $69.1\%$-positive test set.

\subsection{False-positive adjudication prompt}
\label{app:judge}

The $412$ false positives of \S\ref{sec:falsepos} were adjudicated by presenting
them to ChatGPT in a single batch, reportedly GPT-5.6, though the version was
not logged at the time and the authors are not certain of it. This is a
reproducibility gap and the run should be repeated against a recorded model. The
prompt was (reproduced verbatim, including its punctuation):

\begin{quote}\small
I have 412 cases where a language model was supposed to complete a phrase (a
well-known idiom, movie title, or landmark name) but its actual output didn't
exactly match the target string. For each one, I have the target (the correct
completion) and what the model actually generated. I want you to classify each
case into exactly one of three categories:

\textbf{Valid alternate} --- the model's answer means the same thing or is a
genuinely acceptable variant (a synonym, a common alternate phrasing of the same
idiom, a spelling variant, a grammatically different but equivalent form, or a
very reasonable related answer like a sequel number).

\textbf{Partially right} --- the model got some of it right but changed or
dropped something meaningful, so it's not quite equivalent but clearly shows
real knowledge of the target.

\textbf{Unrelated} --- the model's output has no real connection to the target;
it looks like genuine confusion.

For each of the 412 lines below, respond with just the number and the category
(e.g., ``1. Valid alternate''). At the end, give me a total count and percentage
for each of the three categories.
\end{quote}

Each case was supplied on one line as
\texttt{N. PHRASE: <phrase> | TARGET: <target> | GENERATED: <generated text>}.
The rubric wording, in particular the instruction that a variant counts as
valid, plainly invites a generous reading; the resulting split should be taken
as an upper bound on how many of these are not real errors.

\subsection{An efficiency pilot}

Separately from this paper we reproduced CLP \citep{xie-zhou-2026-clp}, a
learned linear read of the hidden state that sets how many drafted tokens to
accept at each step, on Qwen3.5-2B. It reached roughly $1.34\times$ the
throughput of plain autoregressive decoding; a policy that simply drafts a fixed
number of tokens every step was faster still, at $1.72\times$. The test set was
ten held-out prompts, all that were available, so it settles nothing.

Three caveats. Our figure exceeds the $1.14\times$--$1.29\times$ Xie and Zhou
report, which we take to reflect differences in model, hardware and workload
rather than a better implementation. Divergences from autoregressive output
appeared under \emph{every} drafting policy we tried, including fixed-length
ones with no adaptive logic, pointing at this model's interaction with the
inference engine rather than at any policy. And they were more common for the
aggressive fixed-length policies (six of ten prompts against two for CLP), so
the fixed-length speed advantage is not free. We report the pilot only because
it bounds how much the anticipation we measure might be worth in practice.
