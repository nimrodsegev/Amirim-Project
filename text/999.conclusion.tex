\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked whether a language model's internal state, partway through a familiar
multi-word expression, already encodes the rest. For fixed expressions in
natural text the answer is yes, and at a useful range: OLMo-2-7B completes such
phrases three tokens early in roughly two thirds of instances and five tokens
early in more than half. The ability is a stable property of the phrase rather
than of the occasion --- given ten natural contexts, a phrase tends either to
work in almost all of them or in almost none --- and it is legible from the
representation itself, well enough that a small probe reading a single hidden
vector predicts real completion at $87.6\%$ precision.

The second finding is a caution about how the first was obtained. Patchscopes
and ordinary generation, run on identical instances, agree on only $78.7\%$ of
them; they reverse the ranking of the phrase categories, and probes trained on
their labels locate the relevant information in different parts of the network.
Part of that gap is a budget we chose --- widening the Patchscopes sweep from
eight layers to all $32$ recovers six to eight points and rebalances the
asymmetry --- but the disagreement itself survives the widest sweep we can run.
Neither instrument is wrong, and neither is a neutral window onto what the model
knows. Claims of the form \emph{the model already knows $X$} should carry the
readout that produced them, and the budget that readout was given.

The practical thread we have not pulled is efficiency. A signal that is stable
per phrase, available from mid-network states, and precise enough to act on is
the kind of signal a decoding scheme could use to commit several tokens at once
rather than paying full depth for each. We are pursuing that direction
separately, as an adaptive multi-token prediction policy evaluated against an
autoregressive baseline under exact verification; nothing in the present paper
depends on its outcome. An early pilot there is instructive about the size of
the prize: a confidence-gated draft-length policy reached roughly $1.34\times$
the throughput of plain autoregressive decoding, but did not beat a far simpler
policy that always drafts a fixed number of tokens ahead. On that evidence the
open question is not whether anticipation exists --- this paper says it does ---
but whether reading it out adaptively is worth more than not bothering.
