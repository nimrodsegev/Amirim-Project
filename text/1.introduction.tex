\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A decoder-only language model emits one token per forward pass, paying the full
depth of the network for each. This is not always necessary. Some continuations
are all but fixed once the model has read a few tokens: after \textit{the
Empire}, in a sentence about New York, little other than \textit{State Building}
can follow. If the state at \textit{Empire} already holds \textit{State
Building}, the two passes that produce those tokens are recomputing something
the model already had (Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}).

Recent interpretability work has shown that language models do build
representations of units larger than a single token. \citet{kaplan-etal-2025-tokens}
demonstrate an \emph{intrinsic detokenization} process: when a word is split
into several subword tokens, information from the earlier pieces is aggregated
into the last one, and the resulting state behaves like a representation of the
whole word, even for words the tokenizer never emits as a unit. But that is a unit the model has
\emph{already finished reading}: it shows the model pulling earlier evidence
together, not looking ahead. Ours is the forward question, and it is the one
that matters for how models are run:

\begin{quote}
Given a partial phrase, does the model's internal representation already carry
enough information to recover the rest, before it has read or generated all of
it?
\end{quote}

A general version of this question has already been answered yes.
\citet{pal-etal-2023-future} show that tokens two or more positions ahead can be
recovered from a single hidden state, by transplanting it into a fresh context
and reading what the model produces. But they compare the recovered tokens
against whatever the model itself would have written next. There is no target
fixed in advance, so one cannot ask whether the same material comes back on a
different occasion, or whether the model's own behaviour matches it.

We therefore study \emph{fixed multi-word expressions}: idioms, landmark names
and film titles. Here the continuation is set by the phrase, not by the model,
and that buys three things. The distance we measure counts something real, the
tokens left in a known phrase. The same phrase can be tested in many different
sentences, so we can ask whether what varies is the phrase or the occasion. And
because the target is fixed, we can check the model's own writing against it.
These phrases are also common in pretraining text, which makes them the easiest
case: if anticipation does not show up here, it is unlikely to show up
anywhere.
We index the question by \emph{lookahead distance} $i$: the number of phrase
tokens still unread at the point where we interrupt the model. At $i=4$ in
\textit{The Lord of the Rings}, the model has read only \textit{The}, and we ask
whether \textit{Lord of the Rings} is recoverable from its state.

How we look turns out to matter as much as what we find. We use two readouts on
the same interrupted state. The first is
Patchscopes \citep{ghandeharioun-etal-2024-patchscopes}: we lift the hidden
state at the cut point, drop it into an unrelated prompt, and see whether the
model writes out the missing tokens. The second simply lets the model carry on
from the text it has actually read. The first asks what a single vector can be
made to say; the second asks what the model does. A successful Patchscopes
readout is often treated as proof that the model ``knows'' something, and we
wanted to see how far that holds.

Our findings are as follows. Anticipation at the phrase level is substantial:
in natural context, OLMo-2-7B produces the final two tokens of a familiar phrase
in $84.0\%$ of instances and the final five in $53.6\%$, against $59.2\%$ and
$17.6\%$ for the same phrases in isolation (\S\ref{sec:lookahead}). Which phrase it is matters far more than which sentence it sits in: a phrase
tends to be recovered in nearly all of its contexts or in nearly none
(\S\ref{sec:stability}). And the signal can be read off the state directly. A
small classifier looking at one hidden vector predicts whether the model will
finish the phrase, at $76.8\%$ balanced accuracy and $87.6\%$ precision
(\S\ref{sec:probe}).

The two readouts, however, do not measure the same thing, and where they part
they part in one direction. On identical instances, generation recovers
continuations Patchscopes misses about three times as often as the reverse, so a
Patchscopes rate is better read as a floor than as an estimate
(\S\ref{sec:disagreement}). Part of even that gap is a choice rather than a
finding: widening the Patchscopes sweep from eight layers to all $32$ recovers
six to eight points. What does not wash out is where the signal appears to live.
Train the same probe on one readout's labels and it peaks in the middle of the
network; train it on the other's and it peaks at the last layer. We take this as a
warning rather than a refutation. Patchscopes still tells us something real
about what a state holds, but a claim of the form \emph{the model knows $X$ at
layer $\ell$} depends on how it was measured, and should say which readout
produced it and over how many layers.
