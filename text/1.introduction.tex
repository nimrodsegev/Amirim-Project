\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A decoder-only language model emits one token per forward pass, paying the full
depth of the network for each. That is not always necessary. Some continuations
are highly predictable once the model has read a few tokens: after \textit{the
Empire}, in a sentence about New York, little other than \textit{State Building}
can follow. If the model's state at \textit{Empire} already holds \textit{State
Building}, then the two passes that produce those tokens repeat work the model
has already done (Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}).

Whether models really hold such information is a question prior work has answered
only in part. Language models are known to build representations of units larger
than a single token: \citet{kaplan-etal-2025-tokens} show that when a word is
split into several subword pieces, information from the earlier pieces is
gathered into the last one, so that state behaves like a representation of the
whole word. But that is a unit the model has already finished reading. Looking
forward instead, \citet{pal-etal-2023-future} show that tokens two or more
positions ahead can be recovered from a single hidden state, by copying that
state into a fresh context and reading what the model writes. Their target,
though, is whatever the model itself would have written next, which is not fixed
in advance. So one cannot ask whether the same material comes back on a different
occasion, or whether the model's own behaviour matches it. That is the gap we
address:

\begin{quote}
Given a partial phrase, does the model's internal representation already carry
enough information to recover the rest, before it has read or generated all of
it?
\end{quote}

We study this through fixed multi-word expressions: idioms, landmark names and
film titles. These are a good place to look for three reasons. They are common in
pretraining text, so the model has had ample opportunity to learn them. The same
expression turns up in many different sentences, which lets us ask whether what
varies is the phrase or the occasion. And they mean something as a whole, beyond
the words that make them up, so the continuation is set by the expression rather
than chosen by the model. That gives us a target fixed in advance to check
against.

We index the question by \emph{lookahead distance} $i$: the number of phrase
tokens still unread at the point where we interrupt the model. At $i=4$ in
\textit{The Lord of the Rings}, the model has read only \textit{The}, and we ask
whether \textit{Lord of the Rings} is recoverable from its state. To answer that
we use Patchscopes \citep{ghandeharioun-etal-2024-patchscopes}: we lift the
hidden state at the cut point, copy it into an unrelated prompt, and see whether
the model writes out the missing tokens. As a baseline for what the model
actually does with the same input, we also let it simply carry on from the text
it has read.

Our findings are as follows. The rest of a familiar phrase is often recoverable
several tokens early. Over $1.1$k expressions in $9.9$k naturally occurring
sentences, Patchscopes recovers the remaining tokens of a phrase in $57.5\%$ of
instances three tokens before it ends, and in $38.7\%$ five tokens before. The
same phrases shown with no surrounding sentence are recovered far less often, so
how well a model handles these expressions cannot be judged from the phrase alone
(\S\ref{sec:lookahead}). Which phrase it is matters far more than which sentence
it sits in: a phrase tends to be recovered in nearly all of its contexts or in
nearly none (\S\ref{sec:stability}). And the signal can be read off the state
directly, with no intervention at all. A small classifier looking at one hidden
vector predicts whether the model will finish the phrase, at $76.8\%$ balanced
accuracy and $87.6\%$ precision (\S\ref{sec:probe}).
