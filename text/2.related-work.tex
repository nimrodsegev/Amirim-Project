\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Representations of units larger than a token.}
The closest antecedent to this work is \citet{kaplan-etal-2025-tokens}, who show
that when a word is split into several subword tokens, early attention
aggregates the pieces into the final subtoken and feed-forward layers then write
a whole-word representation into that position. They read these states out with
Patchscopes and find that the model can regenerate words for which no single
vocabulary token exists, which they interpret as a latent lexicon broader than
the tokenizer's vocabulary. Their unit is a word the model has finished reading;
ours is a phrase it has not. The two questions share a method but not a claim:
consolidating evidence already received is a weaker property than anticipating
evidence not yet received. \citet{reif-etal-2026-vocab} build on the same
representational picture to reshape model vocabularies, and
\citet{artzy-schwartz-2024-attend} give complementary evidence that attention
gathers information early and later layers operate on the consolidated state.

\paragraph{Idioms and memorised sequences.}
\citet{haviv-etal-2023-understanding} study how transformers recall memorised
idiom completions, locating an early-layer stage that promotes the memorised
continuation and a later stage that refines it. Their focus is the mechanism of
recall for a single target token; we measure how far ahead recall extends and
how it varies across phrases and contexts. Our idiom set is drawn from
MAGPIE \citep{haagsma-etal-2020-magpie} and
LIdioms \citep{moussallem-etal-2018-lidioms}.

\paragraph{Reading hidden states.}
The logit lens \citep{nostalgebraist-2020-logitlens} projects an intermediate
state through the unembedding to ask what token it would predict.
Patchscopes \citep{ghandeharioun-etal-2024-patchscopes} generalises this by
injecting the state into an arbitrary prompt and letting the model verbalise it,
which removes the constraint that the answer be a single vocabulary item and
makes multi-token readouts possible. Both are inference-time interventions on a
representation, and both raise the question of whether what is extracted is what
the model would otherwise have used. We address that question directly by
running Patchscopes and ordinary generation on identical inputs
(\S\ref{sec:disagreement}); to our knowledge this comparison has not been
reported at scale.

\paragraph{Probing internal states for future correctness.}
\citet{orgad-etal-2025-llms} show that LLM hidden states encode substantially
more about the truthfulness of a forthcoming answer than the output
distribution reveals, that this signal is concentrated in particular tokens, and
that probes trained on it generalise poorly across datasets. Our probe is the
same shape of object applied to a different target --- not whether an answer
will be true, but whether a phrase will be completed --- and we find a
comparable pattern, including the limited cross-category generalisation reported
in Appendix~\ref{app:details}. \citet{gekhman-etal-2024-fine} similarly treat a
model's pre-existing knowledge of an item as a measurable quantity that predicts
downstream behaviour.

\paragraph{Exploiting anticipation for efficiency.}
If a model's state already determines several upcoming tokens, that redundancy
can be spent. Speculative decoding \citep{leviathan-etal-2023-fast} drafts
several tokens with a cheaper process and verifies them with the target model;
multi-token prediction heads \citep{gloeckle-etal-2024-better} train the model
to emit several tokens at once; and LayerSkip
\citep{elhoushi-etal-2024-layerskip} exits early and self-speculates.
\citet{aynetdinov-akbik-2025-babies} study when such lookahead ability emerges
during training. These methods assume that useful anticipation exists; our
contribution is to measure it directly, for one well-defined class of
continuations, and to characterise when it is reliable. We do not implement a
decoding method here, and we report no speed-ups.
