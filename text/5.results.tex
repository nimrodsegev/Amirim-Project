\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We begin with the central measurement: how often the rest of a phrase can be
recovered at each lookahead distance, with and without the surrounding sentence
(\S\ref{sec:lookahead}). We then ask whether recovery is spread evenly across
phrases or concentrated in some of them (\S\ref{sec:stability}), and how far our
two measurements agree with each other (\S\ref{sec:disagreement}). Finally we
ask whether a small classifier reading a single hidden state can tell in advance
whether the model will get the phrase right (\S\ref{sec:probe}), and what the
cases it gets wrong look like (\S\ref{sec:falsepos}).

\subsection{Phrases are recovered several tokens ahead}
\label{sec:lookahead}

\input{tables/main_results}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/context_vs_isolation}
\caption{Recovery rate against lookahead distance $i$. Solid curves are phrases
in their original sentences; dashed curves are the same phrases presented alone.
Both Patchscopes curves sweep all $32$ layers, so the context comparison is like
for like; the eight-layer budget used elsewhere in the paper appears in
Table~\ref{tab:main}. Recovery falls as $i$ grows, and context raises it
substantially at every distance.}
\label{fig:context}
\end{figure}

We interrupt OLMo-2-7B inside each phrase, leaving $i$ tokens unread, and let it
continue greedily for $i+3$ tokens from the prefix it has actually seen. The
trial succeeds if the unread tokens appear in that continuation.
Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the result. With the phrase in its original sentence,
the remaining tokens appear in $84.0\%$ of instances at $i=2$, $64.8\%$ at $i=3$
and $53.6\%$ at $i=5$. So this is not a small effect that dies out after one
token: it holds several tokens ahead, well past ordinary next-token prediction.

\paragraph{Isolation understates the effect.}
The same phrases presented alone, with no surrounding text, are recovered far
less often: $59.2\%$ at $i=2$ falling to $17.6\%$ at $i=5$ (Figure~\ref{fig:context}).
The generation gap widens with distance, from $24.8$ points at $i=2$ to $36.0$
at $i=5$. Patchscopes benefits substantially too, by $21.8$ to $33.2$ points,
though not in a steadily widening way. The direction is no surprise: context gives the model both the topic and the
cue that a set phrase is under way. The size is what matters. A bare phrase is a
poor stand-in for how a model actually meets these expressions, and for these
phrases and models, testing in isolation badly understates what the model can
do.

\paragraph{In isolation the two measurements nearly coincide.}
The lower pair of curves in Figure~\ref{fig:context} is the isolated condition,
and the two track each other within a point or two at every distance:
$57.8\%$ against $59.2\%$ at $i=2$, $16.5\%$ against $17.6\%$ at $i=5$. Whatever
separates them in context (\S\ref{sec:disagreement}) does not appear when the
phrase stands alone, itself a clue that the disagreement is something context
creates rather than something fixed about how we measure.

\paragraph{Successes persist beyond the range we analyse.}
We stop at $i=5$ because few phrases are long enough to go further: only $185$
phrases reach $i=6$ and $51$ reach $i=8$. Recovery does not stop there. In
context, generation recovers $48.9\%$ of the $1{,}123$ trials at $i=6$ and
$41.7\%$ of the $312$ at $i=8$. These samples are small, so we report them for
completeness and base no claims on them.

\subsection{Recovery varies systematically by phrase}
\label{sec:stability}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/per_phrase_consistency}
\caption{Share of its own distinct contexts in which each phrase's continuation
is recovered at $i=3$ by generation, over the $803$ phrases with at least
five distinct contexts. Grey is the binomial distribution expected if every
context were an independent draw at the pooled rate $p=0.64$. The two extreme
bins are kept separate because most phrases have exactly five distinct contexts.
The observed distribution has mass at both ends, where the null has almost
none, and is thin in the middle, where the null concentrates.}
\label{fig:consistency}
\end{figure}

An aggregate rate of $64.8\%$ at $i=3$ is consistent with two very different
pictures. Either the model recovers most phrases most of the time with some
per-occasion noise, or it reliably recovers some phrases and reliably fails on
others. Only the second supports the idea of a stable, reusable phrase-level
representation.

For each of the $803$ phrases with at least five distinct contexts
(\S\ref{sec:setup}) we compute the share of its contexts that succeed, and
compare the resulting distribution against the binomial null in which every
context is an independent draw at the pooled rate $p = 0.645$
(Figure~\ref{fig:consistency}). The two differ sharply. $31.5\%$ of phrases are
recovered in \emph{every} context, where the null predicts $10.1\%$, and
$7.7\%$ in none at all, where it predicts $0.5\%$. The null instead concentrates
in the middle, putting $56.0\%$ of its mass in the two central bins against
$32.3\%$ observed. Patchscopes is more extreme still, with $37.4\%$
of phrases recovered in at least $90\%$ of their contexts against a null of
$9.5\%$.

So the differences between phrases are far larger than they would be if every
context were an independent coin flip at the same rate. Some expressions are
recovered in most of their occurrences and others in almost none. That does not show the phrase \emph{itself} is what matters, because we have
not separated a phrase from the things that travel with it: how often it appears
in pretraining, how long it is, how it is tokenized, what category it belongs
to, and how alike its contexts happen to be (\S\ref{sec:limitations}). What it does show is that
outcomes group tightly by phrase: sentences containing the same expression
behave much more alike than chance would predict. Whether that is the phrase
itself or something that comes with it remains open. Either way it is what makes
the probing question in \S\ref{sec:probe} worth asking: a signal that is
consistent within a phrase is one that something in the representation could
carry.

\paragraph{Where in the model it becomes readable.}
Recording Patchscopes success at each probed layer separately gives the same
shape at every distance: recovery is low early, rises through the middle of the
network, peaks at L25 and falls back at L30 (Appendix~\ref{app:details}). The
late decline may be the last layers specialising toward the actual next token
rather than maintaining a transferable representation, consistent with the
decline \citet{kaplan-etal-2025-tokens} report for words, though we do not
establish it. Over all $32$ layers the median first successful layer is L16 and
$41.9\%$ of successes appear at or before L10, so phrase information becomes
readable gradually and, for a large minority, early.

\subsection{Patchscopes and generation do not always agree}
\label{sec:disagreement}

\input{tables/categories}

We have so far reported the two measurements side by side. Here we compare them
directly, on identical instances: the same phrase, the same context, the same
cut point and the same success criterion. They agree on $78.7\%$ of the
$24{,}740$ pooled trials (Table~\ref{tab:agreement}). The disagreement is
lopsided: generation recovers a continuation that Patchscopes misses in
$15.7\%$ of instances, while Patchscopes recovers one that generation misses in
only $5.6\%$.

Much of that gap comes from how many layers we gave Patchscopes. The comparison
above uses eight. Sweeping all $32$ raises its in-context rate from $73.2\%$ to
$79.6\%$ at $i=2$ and from $38.7\%$ to $46.4\%$ at $i=5$ (Table~\ref{tab:main}),
lifts agreement to $81.1\%$, and brings the disagreement close to even; at $i=3$
the two then differ by $0.7$ points overall. Some disagreement remains at $32$
layers, with $18.9\%$ of instances classified differently and $7.8\%$ recovered
only by Patchscopes. We keep the eight-layer sweep as our primary measurement
because every per-layer, per-category and probing analysis was run on that layer
set. Readers comparing our Patchscopes rates against other work should note that
the budget moves them by six to eight points.\nimrod{Worth discussing: when
Patchscopes fails and generation succeeds, we cannot tell from these
measurements whether the intervention missed something the state holds or the
model simply was not looking ahead at that point. I have not tried to settle it
here.}

The examples in Table~\ref{tab:disagree} suggest the two kinds of disagreement
differ in character, though they are illustrative rather than a systematic
sample. Where Patchscopes succeeds and generation does not, the intervention
brings the phrase out of the state while the surrounding context steers
generation elsewhere: given \textit{Why should one person be brought up}, the
model writes \textit{a wealthy family, with every opportunity} rather than
\textit{in the lap of luxury}. That is a different continuation, not a wrong
one, and a reminder that our criterion asks for a specific string rather than a
good completion. Where generation succeeds and Patchscopes does not, the
patchscope reaches for a neighbour: for \textit{a bird in the hand is worth two
in the bush} it returns \textit{worth more than one}, recovering the sense and
losing the wording.

\paragraph{The two rank phrase categories differently.}
Table~\ref{tab:categories} breaks the pooled rates down by phrase kind. Under
Patchscopes, idioms are the \emph{easiest} category at $57.4\%$, ahead of film
titles ($54.7\%$) and landmarks ($47.2\%$). Under generation, idioms are the
\emph{hardest} at $66.4\%$, behind film titles ($84.5\%$) and landmarks
($75.3\%$). Under the wider $32$-layer sweep idioms sit in the middle of the
Patchscopes ranking rather than at the top, so the difference narrows but does
not disappear. One possible explanation, which we have not tested, is the
scoring artifact of \S\ref{sec:falsepos}: idioms have accepted variants, so
exact matching would penalise them most where the continuations are most fluent.

\subsection{The hidden state predicts generation success}
\label{sec:probe}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/probe_balanced_accuracy}
\caption{Balanced accuracy of the two probes by layer. Each probe reads a single
hidden state and predicts whether recovery succeeds; the two differ only in
which measurement supplies the label. The grey line is the raw accuracy a
majority-class guess would reach on the Patchscopes label. It is shown because
that label's positive rate moves sharply with depth, from $93.4\%$ at L5 to
$52.3\%$ at L25, which is why we report balanced accuracy rather than raw
accuracy.}
\label{fig:probes}
\end{figure}

If the model is really looking ahead, that should be visible in the hidden state
itself, without any intervention. We train a small MLP on the raw hidden state at
the cut position to predict whether recovery succeeds, pooling
$i \in \{2,3,4,5\}$ and splitting by phrase so that no test phrase is seen in
training (Appendix~\ref{app:details}). We train two versions, differing only in
which measurement supplies the label.

Predicting \emph{generation success} works well. Balanced accuracy trends
upward with depth, from $69.8\%$ at L5 to a maximum of $76.8\%$ at L30 with a
small dip at L20, and precision
is high and nearly flat at $83.3\%$ to $87.6\%$ (full metrics in
Appendix~\ref{app:details}). A
positive call from this probe is right roughly seven times in eight, and it is
made from a single hidden vector, before the model has produced any of the
tokens in question.

\paragraph{The signal is learned, not a base rate.}
Because $69.1\%$ of instances are positive, a probe could score respectably by
guessing. Balanced accuracy already corrects for that, and running the same
probe on the earliest layers gives a clean gradient: $58.6\%$ at L0, the raw
embedding, rising through $65.1\%$ at L1 and $66.5\%$ at L2 to $69.8\%$ at
L5 (Appendix~\ref{app:details}). A base-rate artifact would be flat; this
climbs as computation accumulates. That L0 is already above chance does suggest
part of the signal is lexical identity of the cut token rather than context.

\paragraph{The label changes where the ``best'' layer is.}
Training the identical architecture on the Patchscopes label instead produces a
different profile: balanced accuracy peaks in the middle of the network
($76.5\%$ at L10, $76.3\%$ at L15) and is worst at L30 ($66.4\%$), the mirror
image of the generation-label curve (Figure~\ref{fig:probes}). Raw accuracy for
this probe is not comparable across layers at all, because the positive rate
moves from $6.6\%$ at L5 to $47.7\%$ at L25 as Patchscopes itself gets better.
A reader interested in \emph{where in the model phrase information lives} would
draw opposite conclusions from the two probes. We read the generation-label
probe as the more trustworthy of the two, since its label does not depend on the
layer being probed.

The same mid-network peak appears when we ask whether the Patchscopes-label
probe tracks phrase identity rather than a general notion of difficulty.
Comparing each held-out phrase's mean predicted probability with its true
success rate, the correlation peaks at $r=0.62$ (L10) and falls away at both
ends, to $0.41$ at L5 and $0.48$ at L30 (Appendix~\ref{app:details}). We have
not run the equivalent analysis on the generation-label features, so this says
where \emph{Patchscopes} success becomes phrase-predictable, not where the model's
own behaviour does.

\subsection{Most apparent errors are valid alternates}
\label{sec:falsepos}

Some of what we count as probe mistakes may be mistakes in the scoring rather
than in the probe. We checked all $412$ false positives of the generation-label probe at
L25, cases where the probe predicted completion and exact-match scoring
recorded a failure, by presenting each (target, generated text) pair to
ChatGPT with a three-way rubric (Appendix~\ref{app:judge}). It labelled $259$
cases ($62.9\%$) valid alternates, $72$ ($17.5\%$) partially correct and $81$
($19.7\%$) unrelated. Typical cases are \textit{laughs best} for \textit{laughs
loudest} and \textit{beat around the bush} for \textit{beat about the bush}: the
more common form of the expression, scored as a failure.

This is a single judge, with no human validation and no record of which ChatGPT
version produced it, so we report it as indicative of the direction of the bias
rather than as a corrected accuracy. Read that way, roughly four in five of
these apparent errors reflect the rigidity of exact matching rather than a model
that did not know the phrase. These cases were selected by the probe, not
sampled from all exact-match failures, so this demonstrates a limitation of
exact matching within that subset; it does not establish the direction or size
of any bias in the recovery rates of \S\ref{sec:lookahead}. An independent split
of the same cases by the model's own confidence agrees and uses no judge at all: in $40.0\%$ the model was itself confident ($>0.7$), and
in only $10.4\%$ unsure ($<0.3$).

\paragraph{The model's own confidence tracks the observed outcome.}
Taking the geometric mean of the probability the model assigns to each token it
generates, confidence averages $0.878$ on successful completions and $0.447$ on
failures. Split by probe outcome at L25 it follows the true label rather than
the probe's guess: true positives average $0.904$ and false negatives $0.791$,
against $0.599$ for false positives and $0.414$ for true negatives
(Appendix~\ref{app:details}). When probe prediction and generation outcome
disagree, the model's confidence tracks the outcome more closely than the
prediction, on average. Confidence and probe output may therefore be
complementary signals, though we evaluate no combined decision rule.
