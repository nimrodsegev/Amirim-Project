\begin{abstract}

Language models produce one token per forward pass, but when they write a
familiar multi-word expression, the next few words are largely settled before
they are written. We ask whether this shows up inside the model: partway through
such a phrase, does the hidden state already carry enough information to recover
the words still to come? We test this on OLMo-2-7B with $1.1$k idioms, landmark
names and film titles, stopping the model at a controlled \emph{lookahead
distance} $i$ and reading the hidden state at that point with Patchscopes. Across
$9.9$k collected phrase-context instances, about $5.5$k of them distinct, the
remaining tokens of a phrase are
recovered in $57.5\%$ of instances three tokens before the phrase ends and
$38.7\%$ five tokens before. The same phrases shown with no surrounding sentence
are recovered far less often, so a model meeting these expressions in real text
is in a much better position than an isolated test would suggest. Results group
tightly by phrase: given several natural contexts, a phrase tends to be
recovered in almost all of them or in almost none. The signal can also be read
straight from the state, with no intervention at all: a small classifier reading
a single hidden state predicts whether the model will complete the phrase, at
$87.6\%$ precision. Models therefore carry usable information about words they
have not yet produced, and that information can be detected before those words
appear. That is the kind of advance signal a decoding method would need in order
to commit several tokens at once.
\end{abstract}
