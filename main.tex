\begin{abstract}
Language models emit one token per forward pass, yet some continuations are
effectively fixed several tokens in advance. We ask whether a model's hidden
state, partway through a familiar multi-word expression, already carries enough
information to recover the rest of it. Interrupting OLMo-2-7B at a controlled
\emph{lookahead distance} $i$ across $1{,}164$ idioms, landmark names and film
titles, in isolation and in $9{,}955$ naturally occurring contexts, we find that
it often does: the model produces
the final three tokens of a phrase in $64.8\%$ of in-context instances and the
final five in $53.6\%$, against $31.2\%$ and $17.6\%$ for the same phrases
presented alone. Recovery clusters strongly by phrase: outcomes across a phrase's contexts are
far more extreme than an independent-context model at a common rate predicts. It is also legible from
the representation itself:
a small probe on a single hidden state predicts whether the model will
complete the phrase, at $76.8\%$
balanced accuracy and $87.6\%$ precision. We also set that single-state
intervention against the model's own continuation from the matched prefix. Run
on identical instances the two agree on only $78.7\%$ of them, reverse the
ranking of the phrase categories, and, used as probe labels, locate the relevant
information in different parts of the network; widening the Patchscopes layer
sweep closes part of the gap but not the disagreement. How much anticipation one
measures, and where in the network it appears, both depend strongly on the
readout.
\end{abstract}
