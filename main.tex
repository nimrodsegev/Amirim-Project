\begin{abstract}
Language models emit one token per forward pass, yet some continuations are
effectively fixed several tokens in advance. We ask whether a model's hidden
state, partway through a familiar multi-word expression, already encodes the
rest of it. Interrupting OLMo-2-7B at a controlled \emph{lookahead distance} $i$
across $1{,}164$ idioms, landmark names and film titles, in isolation and in
$9{,}955$ naturally occurring contexts, we find that it does: the model produces
the final three tokens of a phrase in $64.8\%$ of in-context instances and the
final five in $53.6\%$, against $31.2\%$ and $17.6\%$ for the same phrases
presented alone. The ability is a stable property of the phrase rather than of
the occasion --- across a phrase's natural contexts, success is far more
bimodal than independent per-context variation would produce --- and it is
legible from the representation itself:
a small probe on a single hidden state predicts real completion at $76.8\%$
balanced accuracy and $87.6\%$ precision. We also compare two ways of reading
these states. Run on identical instances, Patchscopes and ordinary generation
agree on only $78.7\%$ of them, reverse the ranking of the phrase categories,
and, used as probe labels, locate the relevant information in different parts of
the network; widening the Patchscopes layer sweep closes part of the gap but not
the disagreement. Phrase-level anticipation is
real, stable and decodable; how much of it one measures depends on the
instrument.
\end{abstract}
