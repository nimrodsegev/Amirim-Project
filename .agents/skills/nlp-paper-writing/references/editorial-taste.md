# Editorial taste: deciding what earns space

The source directory records what the project knows. The paper is a designed path to one consequential conclusion. Do not confuse completeness of project history with completeness of scientific reporting.

## Find the editorial center

Before selecting material, identify:

1. the one finding, object, or idea the intended reader should remember;
2. the tension, error, or consequential gap that makes it matter;
3. the minimum evidence needed to establish the conclusion honestly;
4. the likely skeptical question the paper must answer.

If these are unsettled, propose two or three plausible centers and discuss them with the authors before drafting extensively.

## Triage available material

Assign each result, detail, example, and analysis one role:

- **Core:** necessary to understand or believe the central claim. Put it in the main text and make it salient.
- **Supporting:** strengthens scope, mechanism, robustness, or reproducibility but is not needed on first reading. Summarize in the main text when useful; place full detail in an appendix, table, or figure.
- **Boundary:** unfavorable, null, or contradictory evidence that materially changes interpretation. Report it where readers need it; never hide it to preserve a cleaner story.
- **Context:** prior work or background needed to understand the problem. Include only the amount required for this reader and argument.
- **Process:** failed attempts, chronological detours, implementation archaeology, and analyses that do not change the conclusion. Usually omit, unless they teach a general lesson or rule out a live alternative.

Record uncertain assignments instead of silently discarding material.

## Selection tests

For every candidate item, ask:

- What reader question does it answer?
- Which paper claim would weaken or become misleading without it?
- Does it change the conclusion, establish its scope, explain a mechanism, or enable reproduction?
- Is it the clearest or most representative evidence, or merely the most detailed evidence available?
- Does it deserve scarce reader attention more than the material it would displace?

Detail is not importance. Amount of work is not contribution count. Novelty without consequence is not a reason to foreground something.

## Shape the narrative without distorting the science

Present the journey of understanding, not the chronology of the project. A clean argument may omit failed approaches and intermediate designs. It may not omit unflattering experiments that define the method's actual scope or contradict a stated claim.

Do not build suspense. Put the memorable answer early, then layer explanation for skimmers, careful readers, and replicators. Let the table carry comprehensive numbers; use prose for the comparison and magnitude the argument turns on.

Prefer one strong contribution with coherent evidence over a list of loosely connected contributions. When strong material does not fit the editorial center, suggest an appendix, follow-up paper, or separate note rather than forcing it into the main narrative.

## Output before drafting from a large source dump

Provide a short selection memo:

- proposed editorial center;
- core items for the main text;
- supporting items for appendix or compact mention;
- boundary evidence that must remain visible;
- process material to omit;
- unresolved choices for the authors.

Only then build the section or paper outline.
