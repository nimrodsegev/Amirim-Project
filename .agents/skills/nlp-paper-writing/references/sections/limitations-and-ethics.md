# Limitations, ethics, and societal impact

## Job

Tell readers where the paper's conclusions stop and what risks or obligations
follow from the work. Honest limitations strengthen the scientific contract;
they are not a late confession or a place to introduce work that belonged in
the main paper.

## Build from the claim-evidence map

For each central claim, inspect assumptions, sampled languages or populations,
datasets, models, tasks, metrics, compute budgets, access conditions, and
uncontrolled factors. Ask what plausible change would make the conclusion fail
or narrow its use. Include unfavorable evidence that materially changes scope.

Useful categories include:

- construct and proxy mismatch;
- sampling and coverage limits;
- sensitivity to models, prompts, languages, domains, or time;
- confounds and unmatched resources;
- statistical and measurement uncertainty;
- reproducibility or access constraints;
- foreseeable misuse, affected groups, privacy, consent, labor, licensing,
  environmental cost, and unequal benefits or harms.

Use project records, data statements, licenses, annotation protocols, ethics
approvals, and experiment logs rather than generic responsible-AI language.

## Placement and proportion

Put a caveat beside the result when it is necessary to interpret that result;
the dedicated section can synthesize its broader consequence. Give space in
proportion to scientific importance. Do not inflate a small caveat through a
long defensive discussion, but do not hide a central boundary in the appendix.

Separate demonstrated limits from possible future generalization. Future work
is useful when it follows from a named boundary, not as a generic wish list.
Follow the target venue's required titles, placement, checklists, and disclosure
rules.

## Final checks

Would a reader know which uses and generalizations the evidence does not
support? Are risks tied to this system, data, or deployment rather than to AI in
general? Does the abstract or introduction claim more than this section permits?

## Source basis

Primarily *Ever Growing Academic Writing*, PDF pages 3–6 and 25, on keeping
confidence consistent, handling rather than merely acknowledging difficulties,
aligning space with importance, and locating limitations; and Vered Shwartz's
*Tips for Writing NLP Papers*, PDF pages 3–6, on reporting unfavorable results,
not overselling, and presenting limitations as part of honest scholarship.
Venue-specific ethics and limitations rules remain authoritative because the
three style sources do not cover every modern disclosure requirement.
