# Background, problem setup, and motivation

## Decide whether the section is needed

Motivation and Background solve different reader problems. Motivation makes the
problem concrete and worth attention. Background supplies definitions, prior
findings, datasets, or systems needed to understand the new work. Problem Setup
formalizes the objects and assumptions on which later claims depend.

Keep these as separate subsections only when each has enough distinct work to
do. Otherwise place a compact motivation in the introduction and definitions at
the start of Method or Experimental Setup.

## Motivation

Choose the motivation most directly supported by the paper's findings. Make the
cost, error, mismatch, or missed capability visible through a representative
example, stakeholder, or small diagnostic result. A new problem may deserve
substantial development; a familiar goal such as improving accuracy or speed
usually does not need several layers of generic importance.

Keep solution mechanics out. End by creating the need for the answer that the
next section provides.

## Background

Include only prerequisites a first-time reader in the intended NLP audience
cannot reasonably supply. Define each concept before use, explain why it matters
to this paper, and keep terminology aligned with later sections. Give prior work
credit, but do not turn Background into a paper-by-paper literature survey.

Keep old and new work visibly separate. Do not introduce the paper's contribution
as if it were established background, or interrupt the new method with extended
history that belongs here or in Related Work.

## Problem setup

Define inputs, outputs, assumptions, evaluation target, and notation needed by
multiple later sections. Use a running example when formal objects are difficult
to visualize. Do not front-load symbols that are used only once or definitions
that the method can introduce locally.

## Source basis

Primarily *Ever Growing Academic Writing*, PDF pages 2, 6–8, and 22–23, on
reader-centered problems, examples, concise motivation, prerequisites, and the
Background/Related Work boundary; Vered Shwartz's *Tips for Writing NLP Papers*,
PDF pages 1–5, on why before how, abstraction before detail, self-containment,
and recurring examples; and *Academic Writing for NLP*, PDF pages 22 and 25, on
keeping old and new work separate and making Background optional and minimal.
