# Figures, tables, and captions

## Job

Make the paper's method or evidence graspable at a glance and verifiable under
careful reading. Every artifact should have one main job: teach the mechanism,
show the decisive comparison, expose a tradeoff, reveal a failure mode, or
provide detailed reference values.

## Build from the source of truth

Generate quantitative artifacts from verified data whenever possible. Record
units, normalization, directionality, aggregation, uncertainty, missing values,
and fixed versus varying conditions. Check that labels, values, colors, and
terminology agree with the prose and other artifacts.

## Design for the reading path

- Give the most important result or concept the strongest visual salience.
- Prefer a figure that reveals one relationship quickly over a dense display of
  everything measured. Put reference grids and exhaustive tables in the appendix.
- Use consistent encodings, scales, names, and precision across related artifacts.
- Make labels meaningful without relying on internal variable names.
- Keep text legible at the paper's rendered size and do not rely on color alone.
- Refer to every artifact from the prose and state the finding it supports.

A first-page figure should earn its space by making the problem, method, or main
result easier to understand. A method diagram should follow one example through
the same stages and names used in the Method section.

## Write self-contained captions

A caption should state what is shown, how to read it, what varies, what remains
fixed, the relevant data or population, and the supported takeaway. Define
nonstandard quantities and disclose subsampling or aggregation needed to
interpret the artifact. Put a central caveat in the caption when readers could
otherwise draw the wrong conclusion.

Begin with the artifact's informative subject rather than “This figure shows.”
Use the surrounding prose to interpret the evidence, not to repeat every value.
Follow venue conventions for caption placement and table styling.

## Source basis

Primarily *Ever Growing Academic Writing*, PDF pages 4, 14, and 26–35, on
salience, number rationing, standalone artifacts, visual attention, graphics,
and captions; Vered Shwartz's *Tips for Writing NLP Papers*, PDF pages 1–2 and
7, on visual method overviews and readable LaTeX tables; and *Academic Writing
for NLP*, PDF pages 24, 26–27, on a one-figure paper summary, stable terminology,
precision, highlights, and explanatory captions.
