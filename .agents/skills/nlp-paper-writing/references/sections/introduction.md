# Introduction

## Job

Prepare a first-time NLP reader to understand and evaluate the paper. By the end,
the reader should know the consequential problem, the paper's answer, the main
evidence, the contribution's scope, and the path through the paper.

## Build the reader's path

Start from a setting the intended reader recognizes, but reach the paper-specific
problem quickly. A useful default is:

1. accepted setting or practice;
2. concrete tension, failure, or cost;
3. the gap or question exposed by that tension;
4. the paper's named answer and intuition;
5. the evidence sequence and headline findings;
6. precise contributions and a developed implication.

A recurring example can carry the reader from the problem through the method
and later analysis. Prefer an error, mismatch, or consequence that the paper
actually resolves over a generic statement that the topic is important.

## Control what enters

- Include prior-work facts needed to establish the problem before presenting the
  answer. Move detailed comparison and comprehensive attribution to Related Work.
- Give background only when the reader cannot understand the answer without it.
- Preview experimental setup only to the extent needed to interpret a finding.
- State the main findings rather than promising an investigation.
- Present contributions in the same conceptual order as the body. Each should
  name what exists or was established and how it is supported.
- Use section pointers when they help skimmers or reveal the argument's order;
  do not add a mechanical table of contents.

When results are expected but not finalized, write the intended claim with
visible placeholders for unresolved numbers or scope. Do not turn the whole
paper hypothetical unless the result direction is genuinely unknown.

## Diagnose weak introductions

Cut opening paragraphs until reaching the first one the paper cannot do without.
If the opening could introduce many neighboring papers unchanged, make the
problem or consequence more specific. If the introduction follows project
chronology, reorder it into the reader's journey of understanding. If it ends in
a slogan, develop the consequence supported by the paper's evidence.

## Source basis

Primarily *Ever Growing Academic Writing*, PDF pages 2–6 and 21–22, on opening
with the reader's problem, finding the opening by cutting, summarizing the full
paper, avoiding suspense, and linking sections; Vered Shwartz's *Tips for
Writing NLP Papers*, PDF pages 1–5, on answering why before how, writing from
abstract to concrete, self-containment, examples, selectivity, and honest
contribution framing; and *Academic Writing for NLP*, PDF pages 18 and 24–25,
on the first-page summary and reaching “In this paper” quickly.
