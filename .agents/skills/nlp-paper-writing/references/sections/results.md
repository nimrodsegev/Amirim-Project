# Results

## Job

State what the experiments establish. Results should let a skimming reader find
the main outcome quickly and let a careful reader trace every claim to a table,
figure, subset, or verified computation.

## Write from the evidence

Inspect raw and processed result files before drafting. Establish units,
directionality, aggregation, missing-value meaning, seeds or samples, uncertainty,
and the exact baseline. Recompute values when needed. Never treat an exemplar,
old draft, or table caption as the numerical source of truth.

## Organize each result

A useful local sequence is:

1. the question or comparison;
2. only the setup details unique to this experiment;
3. the headline observation and magnitude;
4. the exact supporting artifact;
5. interpretation that follows directly from the observation;
6. qualification, exception, or handoff to analysis.

Lead with the finding rather than the procedure. State important trends in words
even when they are visually obvious, but do not recite every cell from the table.
Let the artifact carry comprehensive values and use prose for the comparison on
which the argument turns.

## Calibrate the claim

Separate observation from explanation. “Accuracy falls on the longest inputs”
is an observation; a proposed mechanism for the fall requires analysis. Match
verbs to evidence: `show` or `find` for established outcomes, `suggest` for
diagnostic support, and `hypothesize` for an untested explanation.

Report uneven, null, and unfavorable results when they constrain the claim. Lead
with the supported central result, then explain important exceptions without
hiding or dramatizing them. Acknowledge an imperfect comparison only together
with its consequence for interpretation.

## Final checks

Do prose, captions, and tables report the same values and conditions? Is the
main finding visible in the first sentences? Does each paragraph state what the
experiment establishes rather than merely that it was run?

## Source basis

Primarily *Ever Growing Academic Writing*, PDF pages 3–6, 14, and 23–24, on
consistent confidence, early findings, number rationing, exact claims, visual
salience, and the Results/Analysis boundary; Vered Shwartz's *Tips for Writing
NLP Papers*, PDF pages 3–5, on selective narrative without hiding unfavorable
experiments and avoiding overselling; and *Academic Writing for NLP*, PDF pages
18 and 27, on finding-first paragraphs and interpreting rather than repeating
tables.
