# Title and terminology

## Job

Make the paper recognizable and accurately searchable. A reader should be able
to retell the contribution using one stable name for its main object, finding,
or distinction.

## Build from evidence

Read the paper brief, contribution statement, and strongest supported result.
Identify the concrete noun phrase that names the paper's main object and the
technical terms that must remain distinct. Do not name the paper after a broader
claim than the evidence supports.

## Shape the title

- Put the subject and distinguishing idea where readers can find them quickly.
- Prefer an informative technical phrase over a generic promise of improvement.
- A memorable phrase, question, or metaphor is useful only when it remains
  aligned with the paper's actual mechanism or finding.
- Preserve important scope markers when omitting them would mislead readers.
- Avoid packing every dataset, metric, and contribution into the title.

## Stabilize terminology

Choose one preferred term for each central object and use it in the title,
abstract, introduction, method, results, and captions. Do not rotate among
`method`, `framework`, `model`, and `architecture` merely for variety. Keep
different terms when they mark a real scientific distinction.

Before finalizing, search the manuscript for abandoned names, inconsistent
capitalization, and changes in the confidence or scope attached to the title's
claim.

## Source basis

Primarily *Ever Growing Academic Writing*, PDF pages 3 and 19–20, on one name
per object, accurate expectations, salience, and title value; supported by
*Academic Writing for NLP*, PDF pages 18 and 24, on leading with the main point
and making the paper graspable in one figure or page.
