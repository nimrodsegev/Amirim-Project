# Abstract

## Job

Give the right reader an accurate, high-density contract for the paper: what
problem matters, what the paper contributes, what evidence it provides, and
what changes if the evidence is accepted. The abstract is both a summary and a
selection device; persuasion must come from specificity rather than promotion.

## Required inputs

Draft from the stable paper brief, method or study description, verified
headline results, and intended scope. Check all numbers and comparison
conditions against their raw source or final table. If these are unsettled, use
explicit placeholders rather than plausible values or vague superlatives.

## Default claim sequence

Use this as a flexible sequence rather than a sentence-count template:

1. Name the topic through a concrete problem, tension, or consequential setting.
2. State the unresolved limitation, error, or question.
3. Introduce the paper's answer and give one intuitive line about it.
4. State what was built, studied, or tested, including decisive scope or controls.
5. Report the headline evidence with its comparison and magnitude when known.
6. End with the supported scientific or practical meaning.

For an analysis paper, the sequence may instead foreground the phenomenon,
prevailing explanation, discriminating test, revised explanation, and breadth.
For a resource paper, foreground what the resource enables and the evidence that
it is reliable or useful.

## Compression rules

- Put the main finding in the abstract; do not build suspense or promise that a
  later section will reveal it.
- Prefer one memorable result with conditions over a list of weak contributions.
- Keep implementation detail only when it is the core idea or necessary to
  interpret the evidence.
- Use the paper's stable terminology and the same confidence level as the body.
- Avoid citations by default when the motivating fact can be stated as context;
  follow venue conventions when attribution is necessary.
- Do not repeat generic importance claims. Let the problem, evidence, and
  implication establish importance.

## Final checks

Can a reader recover the problem, answer, evidence, scope, and meaning without
reading the paper? Does every empirical clause match the results section? Would
the abstract attract readers who need this paper without attracting readers the
paper cannot satisfy?

## Source basis

Primarily *Ever Growing Academic Writing*, PDF pages 20–21, on the abstract as
the paper's most-read compact summary, topic-first openings, findings, and no
suspense; Vered Shwartz's *Tips for Writing NLP Papers*, PDF pages 1–3, on the
abstract as a summary of the introduction and its motivation → gap → work →
findings → implication structure; and *Academic Writing for NLP*, PDF pages
18 and 24, on leading with the result and compressing the paper into one page.
