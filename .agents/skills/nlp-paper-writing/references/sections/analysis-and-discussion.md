# Analysis and discussion

## Job

Use Analysis to test explanations, expose structure, or establish the boundary
of the main result. Use Discussion to synthesize implications, alternatives, and
open questions after the evidence is clear. Neither section is required merely
because it is conventional.

## Build an analysis as an argument

Start with the observation or puzzle that needs explanation. State competing
explanations or a precise hypothesis, then choose an instrument that can
distinguish them: an ablation, intervention, counterfactual, subset analysis,
qualitative example, robustness test, or simple explanatory model. Report the
diagnostic result before interpreting it.

Use this chain when it fits:

`observation → live explanations → discriminating test → diagnostic result →
scoped interpretation`

An association does not establish a mechanism. State what evidence would differ
under competing explanations and calibrate causal language to the intervention.
Retain failures and mixed effects when they alter the explanation or scope.

## Discussion

Discussion may connect the specific finding to a broader conceptual or practical
consequence, revisit assumptions, compare alternative interpretations, and mark
questions the current evidence leaves open. Make the transition from evidence
to interpretation explicit. Do not quietly increase confidence relative to the
Results section or use free-form discussion to introduce unsupported claims.

Keep side analyses only when they explain the central result, define its scope,
answer a serious objection, or reveal a distinct contribution. Otherwise move
supporting detail to the appendix or omit project archaeology.

## Source basis

Primarily *Ever Growing Academic Writing*, PDF pages 3, 23–25, on confidence
consistency, the distinction between Results, Analysis, and Discussion, and
using later sections for deeper interpretation without overclaiming; Vered
Shwartz's *Tips for Writing NLP Papers*, PDF pages 3–5, on recurring examples,
honest negative evidence, and narrative selection; and *Academic Writing for
NLP*, PDF pages 18 and 27, on leading with the finding and using artifacts as
support rather than substituting them for interpretation.
