# Related work

## Job

Place the paper in the literature by giving prior work accurate credit and making
the present contribution's relationship to it precise. The section should
produce a conceptual map, not a shopping list.

## Build from primary sources

Read the cited papers and create compact source notes before drafting. For each
paper, record what it actually contributes, its evaluation scope, relevant
limitations, its relation to the current paper, and page or section pointers.
Use summaries or search only to discover work, then verify claims in the primary
source. Follow `sources-and-evidence.md` and `citations.md`.

## Organize by a positioning question

Choose the smallest set of conceptual axes needed to distinguish the work. For
each cluster:

1. state the shared problem, approach, or capability;
2. synthesize what the cited work establishes;
3. identify the remaining distinction without constructing a straw man;
4. end with the current paper's exact relationship: extension, contrast,
   complementary evidence, different assumption, or different objective.

Use citations next to the smallest factual claim they support. Uncited prose may
express synthesis or the authors' positioning, but not unsupported field facts.
Avoid one sentence per paper when several papers support one proposition.

## Keep the boundary with Background

If readers need a definition or prior result before they can understand the new
method, place it in Background or the introduction. Related Work can then focus
on comparison after the reader understands the contribution. A section near the
front may combine background and related work, but it must still distinguish
prerequisite explanation from positioning.

Do not borrow wording from abstracts. Summarize each work in terminology that is
accurate to the source and useful for the current comparison.

## Source basis

Primarily Vered Shwartz's *Tips for Writing NLP Papers*, PDF pages 6–8, on
reading before citing, source notes, thematic grouping, critical reading,
non-plagiarizing paraphrase, and ACL citation practice; and *Ever Growing
Academic Writing*, PDF pages 22, 24, and 35, on the Background/Related Work
boundary, credit, concrete differentiation, salience, and citation form.
*Academic Writing for NLP*, PDF pages 22 and 28, reinforces separation of old
and new work, but its suggestion that Related Work may be a citation list is not
adopted because it conflicts with the stronger source guidance.
